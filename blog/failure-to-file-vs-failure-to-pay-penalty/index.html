IRS Penalties

Failure-to-File vs. Failure-to-Pay Penalty: What's the Difference?

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

The IRS charges two different penalties when a return is late or tax goes unpaid — and one is much bigger than the other. Knowing the difference explains the most important rule for anyone who's behind: file on time, even if you can't pay.

The two penalties at a glance

Failure to fileFailure to pay
When it appliesYour return is filed lateTax shown on your return isn't paid by the due date
Monthly rate5% of unpaid tax per month or part of a month0.5% of unpaid tax per month or part of a month
Maximum25% of unpaid tax25% of unpaid tax

Failure-to-file: the big one

The failure-to-file penalty is 5% of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month your return is late, up to 25%. If your return is more than 60 days late, there's also a minimum penalty — the lesser of a set dollar amount (adjusted each year for inflation) or 100% of the tax owed.

An extension avoids this penalty, because it pushes back your filing deadline. But an extension doesn't extend the time to pay.

Failure-to-pay: smaller, but it lasts longer

The failure-to-pay penalty is 0.5% of the unpaid tax per month, up to 25%. If you filed on time and set up an IRS payment plan, the rate drops to 0.25% a month while the plan is in effect. If the IRS issues a notice of intent to levy and the balance still isn't paid, it goes up to 1% a month.

When both penalties apply

If you both file and pay late, the failure-to-file penalty is reduced by the failure-to-pay penalty for any month both apply — so the combined charge is 5% a month, not 5.5%.

Interest is added on top

Interest is charged on unpaid tax from the original due date, and on penalties as well. The rate is set every quarter (the federal short-term rate plus 3%) and compounds daily. Unlike penalties, interest generally can't be waived — though if a penalty is removed, the interest on that penalty goes with it.

Late & Unfiled Returns

Can penalties be removed?

Often, yes. The two most common forms of relief:

  • First-Time Abatement. If you have a clean record — generally no significant penalties in the prior three years, and all required returns filed — the IRS may remove failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties for a single tax year.
  • Reasonable cause. If you couldn't file or pay on time despite ordinary care — because of a serious illness, a disaster, or similar circumstances — penalties may be reduced or removed.

The takeaway

Because the failure-to-file penalty is ten times larger per month, filing on time — even if you can't pay — is almost always the right move. Then set up a payment plan and look into penalty relief.

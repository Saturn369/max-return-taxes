The Home Office Deduction: Who Qualifies and How to Claim It

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

If you run a business from home, part of what you spend on your home may be deductible. But the rules are strict, and the right way to claim it depends on how your business is set up. Here's how it works.

First: who can take it

The home office deduction is for self-employed people and business owners. If you're a W-2 employee who works from home for someone else's company, you generally can't deduct home office expenses on your federal return, even if your employer requires you to work remotely.

The two tests your space has to pass

1. Regular and exclusive use

The space must be used regularly for your business, and only for your business. A spare room that's purely an office qualifies. A kitchen table where you also eat dinner doesn't. The space doesn't have to be a full room, but it does need to be a clearly separate area. There are limited exceptions for daycare providers and for storing inventory or product samples.

2. It's your principal place of business, or another qualifying use

The space must meet one of these:

  • It's your principal place of business. This includes a home office where you do your administrative or management work, like billing, scheduling and bookkeeping, if you have no other fixed location where you do that work. So a contractor who works on job sites can still qualify.
  • You meet clients or customers there in the normal course of business.
  • It's a separate structure, like a detached garage or studio, used for your business.

To find your business percentage for the regular method, divide the office's square footage by your home's total square footage. A 200 sq ft office in a 2,000 sq ft home is 10%. Expenses that apply only to the office, like painting it, are fully deductible.

You can choose either method each year. The regular method often produces a bigger deduction, especially for renters and people in high-cost homes. The simplified method is far less work.

The catch: depreciation when you sell

Under the regular method, homeowners deduct depreciation on the office portion of the home. When you later sell the home, the depreciation you claimed, or could have claimed, after May 6, 1997 is generally taxable, even if the rest of your gain is tax-free under the home sale exclusion. That's not a reason to skip the deduction, but it should be part of the decision. The simplified method avoids it entirely.

Own an S-Corp? It works differently

If your business is an S-Corp, you're technically an employee of your own company, so you can't claim a home office deduction on Schedule C. Instead, the corporation can reimburse you for the business share of your home expenses under a written accountable plan. The business deducts the reimbursement, and it isn't taxable income to you. Partners in a partnership may have a similar option, depending on the partnership agreement. (Thinking about becoming an S-Corp? Here's how to decide.)

Keep good records

  • Measurements of the office and your whole home
  • Photos showing the space is set up only for business
  • A year of utility bills, insurance, property tax and mortgage interest or rent statements
  • Receipts for repairs or improvements to the office

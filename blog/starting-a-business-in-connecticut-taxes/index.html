Starting a Business in Connecticut: The Tax Checklist

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

Starting a business in Connecticut comes with a list of tax registrations and ongoing filings. Getting them right at the start is much easier than cleaning them up later. Here's the checklist we walk new business owners through.

1. Choose your structure and how it's taxed

Your legal structure and your tax treatment are two separate decisions.

  • A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship by default, on Schedule C.
  • A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default, filing Form 1065.
  • Either can elect S-Corp status, which can save self-employment tax once profits are high enough. (See should your LLC elect S-Corp status.)

Business entities are formed through the Connecticut Secretary of the State at business.ct.gov.

2. Get an EIN

An Employer Identification Number is your business's federal tax ID. You'll need one to open a business bank account, hire employees, or file as a partnership or corporation. It's free from the IRS at irs.gov. Be careful of websites that charge for it.

3. Register with the Connecticut Department of Revenue Services

If your business needs Connecticut tax accounts, you register with DRS through myconneCT, the state's online tax portal. Common reasons to register:

  • Sales and use tax. If you sell taxable goods or services, you need a sales tax permit before you start selling. Connecticut's general rate is 6.35%, and some items are taxed at different rates or are exempt. Many services are taxable in Connecticut, so check before assuming yours isn't.
  • Employer withholding. If you'll have employees, including yourself as an S-Corp owner, you need a withholding account.

4. If you have employees

  • Register with the Connecticut Department of Labor for unemployment insurance.
  • Set up payroll for federal and Connecticut income tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare, and federal and state unemployment taxes.
  • Collect a Form W-4 and Connecticut Form CT-W4 from each employee, and verify work eligibility with Form I-9.
  • Connecticut also has a Paid Leave program funded by employee payroll contributions, which employers are responsible for withholding and remitting.

5. Keep up with ongoing filings

  • Annual report: Connecticut LLCs and corporations file an annual report with the Secretary of the State to stay in good standing.
  • Sales tax returns: filed monthly, quarterly or annually, depending on how much tax you collect.
  • Payroll filings: federal and state withholding returns, plus year-end W-2s.
  • 1099s for contractors you pay over the threshold. (See the 1099 rules.)
  • Estimated taxes for owners, both federal and Connecticut. (See quarterly estimated taxes.)
  • Annual income tax returns for the business and its owners.
  • Check with your town, too. Connecticut municipalities tax business personal property such as equipment, and generally require a yearly declaration.

6. Set up your records from day one

Open a separate business bank account, use bookkeeping software, and keep receipts. Clean books make every filing above easier, and they're your best defense if the IRS or DRS ever asks questions.

This article is general information, not tax or legal advice for your specific situation. Tax rules change and have exceptions — talk with a tax professional before acting on it.

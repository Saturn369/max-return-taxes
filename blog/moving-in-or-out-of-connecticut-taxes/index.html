Moving In or Out of Connecticut? How Your Taxes Work the Year You Move

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

Moving to or from Connecticut makes the year's taxes more complicated. You'll often need to file in two states, split your income between them, and make sure you're not taxed twice on the same money. Here's how it works.

Which Connecticut return do you file?

Your situationConnecticut return
Lived in Connecticut all yearForm CT-1040, resident return
Moved into or out of Connecticut during the yearForm CT-1040NR/PY, part-year resident return
Lived elsewhere all year but had Connecticut incomeForm CT-1040NR/PY, nonresident return

How a part-year resident's income is split

As a part-year resident, Connecticut generally taxes:

  • All your income from the period you lived in Connecticut, wherever it came from, and
  • Only Connecticut-source income from the period you lived elsewhere, such as wages for work done in Connecticut, Connecticut rental property, or a business operating in Connecticut

Your other state will usually tax the rest the same way. Getting the split right, like which paychecks, bonuses, interest or capital gains fall into which period, is where most part-year mistakes happen.

Avoiding double taxation

When two states could tax the same income, the state where you live generally gives you a credit for tax paid to the other state. That's common for Connecticut residents who commute to work in New York. New York taxes the wages earned there as a nonresident, and Connecticut gives a credit on the resident return. The credit has limits, so you may still owe some tax to Connecticut, but it prevents paying full tax twice.

Remote work makes this trickier. Where your wages are taxed can depend on where you physically did the work and, in some cases, where your employer is located. New York and Connecticut in particular have rules that can surprise people. If you work remotely across state lines, it's worth having someone check.

Moved in or out of Connecticut? We prepare part-year and nonresident returns for both states, and make sure you get credit for tax paid to the other state.

Changing your residency for real

Your tax residency follows your domicile, your true, permanent home. It's not just your mailing address. If you leave Connecticut, especially for a state with no income tax, make sure the move is clear on paper:

  • Get a driver's license and register your car in your new state
  • Register to vote in your new state
  • Update your address with banks, employers, doctors and the IRS
  • Keep records of your move date, lease or home purchase, and where you spend your time

Keeping a home in Connecticut and spending a lot of time here can still make you a Connecticut resident for tax purposes. Under Connecticut's rules, that can happen even if you've technically moved.

Don't forget the rest of the year's details

  • Withholding: make sure your employer updates your state withholding when you move.
  • Estimated payments: self-employed people may need to make estimated payments to both states for part of the year.
  • Business owners: moving yourself doesn't move your business. A Connecticut business can still create Connecticut-source income after you leave.

This article is general information, not tax or legal advice for your specific situation. Tax rules change and have exceptions — talk with a tax professional before acting on it.

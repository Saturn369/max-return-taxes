Should Your LLC Elect S-Corp Status?

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

It's one of the most common questions profitable small-business owners ask: "Should my LLC be an S-Corp?" Sometimes the answer is a clear yes that saves real money every year. Sometimes the extra cost and paperwork outweigh the savings. Here's how to think it through.

How an LLC is taxed by default

A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship, and a multi-member LLC like a partnership. Either way, the owners generally pay self-employment tax — 15.3% for Social Security and Medicare, with the Social Security part capped each year — on their share of the business's net earnings, on top of income tax.

What changes with an S-Corp election

An LLC can elect to be taxed as an S Corporation. The business still passes its income through to the owners, but owners who work in the business are paid in two ways:

  • A salary, run through payroll, which is subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes, just like any employee's wages.
  • Distributions of the remaining profit, which are not subject to self-employment tax.

The potential savings come from that second part: profit taken as distributions avoids the 15.3% tax that would otherwise apply.

The catch: a reasonable salary

The IRS requires S-Corp owner-employees to pay themselves reasonable compensation — roughly what you'd have to pay someone else to do your job. You can't take a token salary and call everything else a distribution. That rule is what decides how much an election can actually save.

The costs of being an S-Corp

  • Running payroll, with quarterly payroll tax filings and year-end W-2s
  • A separate business return (Form 1120-S) every year, due March 15
  • More bookkeeping and accountability for owner pay and distributions
  • Possible state-level costs or filing requirements, depending on your state
  • Health insurance for more-than-2% owners has to be handled through payroll

There can also be effects on other parts of your taxes, like the qualified business income deduction and retirement plan contribution limits. Those should be part of the math.

When it tends to make sense

An S-Corp election generally starts to pay off once your profit is comfortably more than a reasonable salary for your role — enough that the self-employment tax saved on distributions clearly outweighs the added costs. If your profit is modest or swings a lot from year to year, the default treatment may be the better choice.

We can run the numbers for your business — salary, distributions, payroll costs and state rules — and tell you whether an election makes sense.

How and when to elect

The election is made on Form 2553. To take effect for a given year, it generally must be filed no later than two months and 15 days after the start of that tax year — March 15 for a calendar-year business — or any time during the year before. If you miss the deadline, the IRS has procedures for late-election relief in many cases.

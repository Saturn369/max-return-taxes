Late Filing

Haven't Filed Taxes in Years? Here's What Happens — and What to Do

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

If you've gone a year or more without filing, you're far from alone — and the situation is almost always more fixable than it feels. But unfiled returns don't resolve themselves, and waiting usually makes them more expensive. Here's what's actually happening behind the scenes, and how to get current.

What happens when you don't file

Penalties and interest keep growing

If you owe tax, the IRS charges a failure-to-file penalty of 5% of the unpaid tax for each month (or part of a month) the return is late, up to 25%. There's also a smaller failure-to-pay penalty and interest on the balance. The longer a return sits, the more those add up. (We break down the two penalties in this guide.)

Refunds can expire

Plenty of people who don't file are actually owed money. But you generally have three years from the original due date to claim a refund. After that, it's gone — the money stays with the Treasury.

The IRS may file for you — badly

The IRS receives copies of your W-2s, 1099s and other income documents. If you don't file, it can prepare a substitute for return based on that income — without your deductions, credits, dependents, or business expenses. The result is usually a much larger bill than you'd owe on a return you filed yourself. A substitute return doesn't stop you from filing your own; filing your own return is usually how you bring the bill back down.

Collection can follow

Once tax is assessed and notices go unanswered, the IRS can move to collection, including liens and levies on wages or bank accounts. Responding early keeps far more options open.

Self-employed? Your Social Security record is affected too

If you're self-employed, your tax return is how your earnings get reported for Social Security. Years you don't file may not count toward your future benefits.

Late & Unfiled Returns

How to get current, step by step

  1. Find out what the IRS has on file. Your IRS wage and income transcripts show the W-2, 1099 and other income reported for each year — so lost paperwork isn't a dead end. (Here's how to get them.)
  2. Figure out which years to file. The IRS generally expects the last six years of returns to be filed for you to be considered in good standing. That's often the starting point.
  3. Prepare each return properly. Claim every deduction and credit you're entitled to — especially business expenses, which substitute returns leave out.
  4. Don't forget your state. Connecticut and most other states have their own filing requirements, penalties and interest.
  5. Make a plan for any balance. If you can't pay in full, the IRS offers payment plans. And you may qualify for penalty relief, such as First-Time Abatement or reasonable cause.

Should you file even if you can't pay?

Yes. The failure-to-file penalty grows far faster than the failure-to-pay penalty, so filing on its own slows how quickly your balance grows. It also starts the clock on your refund rights and lets you set up a payment plan.

The bottom line

Unfiled returns get more expensive the longer they sit, but they're a solvable problem. The right first step is finding out where you stand — then filing the missing years correctly.

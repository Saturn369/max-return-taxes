Got a Corrected 1099 or K-1 After You Filed? Here's What to Do

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

You filed your return, and then a new form shows up marked "CORRECTED." Maybe your brokerage revised your 1099-B, or a partnership sent an amended K-1 in late summer. Here's how to decide what to do.

Why corrected forms happen

  • Brokerage statements (1099-B, 1099-DIV, 1099-INT) are often revised when funds reclassify their distributions, for example from ordinary dividends to return of capital.
  • Schedule K-1s from partnerships, S-Corps and trusts often arrive late, and sometimes the business itself files an amended return and sends new K-1s.
  • W-2s are corrected on Form W-2c when an employer finds a payroll error.
  • 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC forms get corrected when a payer reported the wrong amount, or sent a form you shouldn't have received.

Step 1: Compare it with what you filed

Put the corrected form next to the original and find exactly what changed. Sometimes nothing that affects your tax changed at all, like a fixed address or account number. In that case, there's nothing to do but keep the form with your records.

Step 2: Decide whether to amend

If the change affects your income, deductions, credits or tax, you generally should file an amended return. That's Form 1040-X for individuals, plus your state amendment.

  • If you'll owe more, amend promptly. Interest runs from the original due date. The IRS also gets a copy of the corrected form, so if you don't amend, you may get a CP2000 notice proposing the change anyway. (See what IRS notices mean.)
  • If you'll get money back, amend to claim it, as long as you're within the refund deadline. That's generally three years from when you filed, or two years from when you paid the tax. (More on amendment time limits.)
  • If the effect is tiny, it's still technically a change to your tax. We'll help you weigh the cost of amending against the amount involved and the chance of an IRS notice.

What if the IRS has a different number than you?

Corrected forms sometimes create mismatches. For example, a corrected 1099-B may report sales proceeds without the cost basis. If you get an IRS notice based on incomplete information, you can respond with your own records, like purchase confirmations and statements, to show the correct gain or loss.

Corrected K-1s: one extra step

A corrected K-1 usually means the business amended its own return. The changes may also affect your state returns, including returns in other states where the business operates. Ask the business which states the correction affects.

How to avoid this next year

  • Don't file the minute your forms arrive if you have brokerage accounts. Corrected 1099s are common in February and March.
  • Wait for every K-1. If you're a partner or S-Corp shareholder and your K-1 won't arrive in time, filing an extension is usually better than filing and amending later. Remember, an extension gives you more time to file, not to pay.
  • Check your IRS transcript before filing to see what's been reported for you. (Here's how to get it.)

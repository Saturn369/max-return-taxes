S-Corp Reasonable Salary: How Much Should You Pay Yourself?

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

The main tax benefit of an S-Corp is that profits paid out as distributions aren't subject to self-employment tax. Your salary is. So every S-Corp owner faces the same question: how much salary do I have to pay myself? The IRS's answer is "reasonable compensation," and getting it wrong can erase the benefit of the S-Corp.

The rule

If you're a shareholder who works in your S-Corp, the IRS expects you to pay yourself a reasonable salary through payroll, with taxes withheld, before taking distributions. Reasonable generally means roughly what the business would have to pay someone else to do the work you do.

There's no fixed formula or percentage in the tax code. You may have heard of a "60/40 rule," splitting profit 60% salary and 40% distributions. It's a rule of thumb, not an IRS rule, and it doesn't protect you if your salary is too low for the work you actually do.

What the IRS looks at

Factors the IRS and the courts have used include:

  • Your training and experience
  • Your duties and responsibilities in the business
  • The time and effort you put into the business
  • What comparable businesses pay for similar services
  • What the business pays non-owner employees
  • The history of your salary compared with distributions
  • How and when bonuses are paid

The risk of paying too little

Paying yourself nothing, or a token salary while taking large distributions, is a classic audit red flag. If the IRS decides your salary was unreasonably low, it can reclassify distributions as wages. The business then owes the payroll taxes that should have been paid, plus penalties and interest.

There's also no need to overpay. Salary above what's reasonable just adds payroll taxes you didn't need to pay. The goal is a figure you can defend.

How to set a salary you can defend

  1. List what you actually do. Many owners wear several hats, like manager, salesperson and technician. Each role has a market rate.
  2. Research comparable pay. Government wage data, such as the Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational surveys, and industry salary surveys are good starting points.
  3. Factor in time. A part-time owner can reasonably earn less than a full-time one.
  4. Write it down. Keep notes on how you arrived at the number and update them as the business changes.

How your salary affects the rest of your taxes

  • Qualified business income (QBI) deduction. Your salary reduces the business income that qualifies for the deduction. At higher incomes, though, the deduction can be limited by the W-2 wages the business pays. A salary that's too low or too high can cost you here.
  • Retirement contributions. Contributions to a Solo 401(k) or SEP for an S-Corp owner are based on W-2 salary, not distributions. A very low salary limits how much you can put away.
  • Social Security. Your future Social Security benefit is based on earnings you paid Social Security tax on. Distributions don't count.

The bottom line

A reasonable salary is part of the cost of having an S-Corp. Set it based on your real role and market pay, document why, and revisit it every year. (Still deciding whether an S-Corp is right for you? Read should your LLC elect S-Corp status.)

Quarterly Estimated Taxes: Who Has to Pay, When, and How Much

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

When you work for an employer, tax comes out of every paycheck. When you work for yourself, nobody withholds it for you. The IRS still expects to be paid during the year, not all at once in April. That's what quarterly estimated taxes are for.

Who has to pay estimated taxes

You generally need to make estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more when you file your return, after subtracting withholding and refundable credits. That usually includes:

  • Sole proprietors, freelancers and independent contractors
  • Partners and LLC members who receive business income
  • S-Corp owners whose payroll withholding doesn't cover their taxes on business profit
  • Anyone with significant investment, rental or other income that has no withholding

Corporations have their own estimated tax rules and generally must pay if they expect to owe $500 or more.

Despite the name, the "quarters" aren't equal: the second covers only two months and the fourth covers four. (See all the year's business dates in our 2027 deadline calendar.)

How much to pay: the safe harbor rules

You avoid the underpayment penalty if your withholding and estimated payments, paid on time, add up to at least the smaller of:

  • 90% of this year's tax, or
  • 100% of last year's tax, or 110% if last year's adjusted gross income was over $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately)

The prior-year rule is the easiest to plan around. Take last year's total tax, divide it by four, and pay that each quarter. Even if your income jumps, you won't owe a penalty, though you'll still owe the difference when you file.

What happens if you underpay

If you pay too little or too late, the IRS charges an underpayment penalty, figured on Form 2210. It works like interest: it's based on how much was short and for how long, at the IRS underpayment rate. Catching up with a bigger payment later in the year reduces the penalty from that point on.

If your income is uneven, such as a business that earns most of its money late in the year, the annualized income method can lower or eliminate the penalty for earlier quarters.

A tip for S-Corp owners and side-gig workers

Federal income tax withholding is treated as if it were paid evenly throughout the year, no matter when it was actually withheld. So if you're behind on estimates, raising the withholding on your S-Corp salary, or on a W-2 job, late in the year can fill the gap and avoid the penalty in a way a late estimated payment can't.

How to pay

  • IRS Direct Pay, free from your bank account at irs.gov
  • Your IRS Online Account, where you can also see payments you've already made
  • EFTPS, the Treasury's free electronic payment system
  • Debit or credit card, through approved processors that charge a fee
  • A check with a Form 1040-ES payment voucher

Don't forget your state

Most states with an income tax, including Connecticut, have their own estimated payment requirements. Connecticut residents use Form CT-1040ES.

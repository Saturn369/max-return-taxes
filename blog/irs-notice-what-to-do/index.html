Got a Letter From the IRS? What It Means and What to Do

November 17, 2026·3 min read·Max Return Taxes

An envelope from the IRS is enough to ruin anyone's day. But most IRS letters are routine, and almost all of them can be resolved if you respond on time. The worst thing you can do is put it in a drawer.

Step 1: Make sure it's real

The IRS generally starts contact by mail. It doesn't open conversations by text message, email or social media, and it never demands payment by gift card, cryptocurrency or wire transfer. If you're unsure whether a letter is real, don't call the number on the letter. Look up the IRS's contact information on irs.gov, or check for the notice in your IRS Online Account.

Step 2: Find the notice number and the deadline

Look in the top or bottom right corner for a code like CP2000 or LT11. That number tells you exactly what kind of letter it is. Then find the response date. Many deadlines are firm, and missing one can cost you rights you can't get back.

Common notices and what they mean

NoticeWhat it meansWhat to do
CP2000Income reported to the IRS doesn't match your return, so the IRS is proposing a change. It's not a bill yet.Agree, or disagree with documents, by the response date
CP14You have a balance due.Pay, or set up a payment plan if you can't pay in full
CP59The IRS has no record of a return it expected from you.File the return, or show that you already filed or didn't need to
CP501 / CP503Reminders that a balance is still unpaid.Pay or arrange a plan before it escalates
CP504Notice of intent to levy. The IRS can take your state tax refund and move toward other collection.Act now: pay, set up a plan or get help
LT11 / Letter 1058Final notice of intent to levy, with your right to a hearing.You generally have 30 days to request a Collection Due Process hearing
Notice of DeficiencyThe IRS's formal determination that you owe more tax.You generally have 90 days to petition the U.S. Tax Court, a deadline that can't be extended

Step 3: Respond, even if you disagree

  • If the IRS is right, pay what you can by the deadline. If you can't pay in full, ask for a payment plan. That stops more serious collection action.
  • If the IRS is wrong, say so in writing with copies of supporting documents, not originals, by the response date. Proposed changes like a CP2000 are often reduced or dropped once the IRS sees the full picture, like the cost basis of stock you sold.
  • If you need more time, contact the IRS before the deadline. Short extensions are often available for routine notices, but not for court deadlines.

Step 4: Keep a paper trail

Keep a copy of the notice, everything you send back and proof of when you sent it, such as certified mail. Write down the date, name and ID number of anyone you speak with at the IRS.

Notices about unfiled returns

If a notice says the IRS has no record of a return, or that it prepared one for you, filing your own accurate return is usually the best response. A return the IRS prepares often leaves out deductions and credits you're entitled to. (Read what happens when you haven't filed.)
